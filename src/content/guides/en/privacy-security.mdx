## Threat Model (3 minutes)

### What Are You Protecting Against?

Security advice only makes sense once you know who you are hiding from. An illustrator who would rather her day job never found her sketch account has a completely different problem from a reporter writing about a government that jails reporters, and the reporter's precautions would make the illustrator's life miserable for no reason at all. So start here.

At the mildest level you simply do not want an employer, a relative or a future client scrolling through everything you have ever posted. The exposure is ordinary: your posts are public, and your profile ties them together into one readable story. Keeping a second identity for the work you would rather not have attached to your name handles most of it.

One step up, you are avoiding particular people. Someone who has doxxed you before, an ex, a pile-on that follows you from platform to platform. Now the account has to be anonymous from its very first post, and it has to stay that way through habit. Security people call those habits OPSEC, short for operational security: the small daily routines that stop one identity leaking into another.

At the top level are whistleblowers, activists working under a hostile state, and anyone whose physical safety depends on never being identified. The adversary there has budget and patience. That means serious OPSEC, keys generated on a device that never touches the internet (air-gapped), and frankly a lot more reading than one guide can give you.

### What Nostr Protects

✅ **Censorship resistance.** No company can ban you, because no company is holding the account. Your identity is a keypair you control, so it cannot be suspended or seized, and your posts sit on whatever relays chose to keep a copy rather than in one firm's database.

✅ **Ownership of your work.** The key is yours. Your profile, your posts and your follower list travel with you to any client that speaks the protocol, and no platform owns the graph of who you talk to.

### What Nostr Doesn't Protect

❌ **Everything is public by default.** Unless it is an encrypted direct message, anyone can read it, and that includes everything wrapped around the post: which relays you use, what time you published, which client you published from. All of it can be collected and analyzed by anyone who cares to.

❌ **Pseudonymous is not the same as anonymous.** Your npub hides your name. It does not hide you. Patterns give people away, and every relay you connect to sees your IP address.

<Callout variant="info">
  **Realistic expectations:** Nostr provides censorship resistance, not perfect
  anonymity. Understand the difference.
</Callout>

## Identity Separation (3 minutes)

### Why Multiple Identities?

Most creators end up wanting **at least two** Nostr identities, and it is far easier to start with two than to try to split one apart later.

The first is your public identity. Real name or working name, the one that goes on a book cover or a gallery wall. It collects reputation slowly, over years, so you handle it carefully.

The second is pseudonymous. Nothing on it points back to your legal name. That is where the half-formed opinions live, the fandom account, the political argument you would rather a client never read, and the throwaway you use to try an unfamiliar app before trusting it with anything valuable.

### When to Separate

Keep two identities apart whenever one half of your life would embarrass the other: personal against professional, hobbies against work, politics against clients. Separate also when you are testing software you have not vetted. And separate when money is involved, since a Lightning address bolted onto a pseudonymous account can quietly connect it to a name.

You can live with a single account if the people around you already know everything anyway, if the stakes are genuinely low, or if you are a public figure whose whole job is being recognized.

### Managing Multiple Keys

The simplest arrangement is one client per identity. Damus for the public account, Amethyst for the private one. Costs nothing to set up, and it stops working the moment you want both on the same phone.

Better is a signer app, which the next section covers. Amber on Android holds several keys at once and gives each client only the signature it asked for.

The strictest version is separate hardware: phone for one identity, an old tablet or laptop for the other, no overlap ever. Tedious to keep up, and the only arrangement that still holds when a device is stolen.

<Callout variant="success">
  **Start simple:** Create one pseudonymous account first. Add a public one
  later if needed.
</Callout>

## Signer Apps (3 minutes)

### What Are Signers?

A **signer app** is a small app whose only job is to hold your nsec and sign on its behalf. The client you post from never receives the key. It hands the signer a draft, the signer shows you what is about to be signed, you approve, and the finished signed event goes back to the client.

### Benefits

✅ **Key isolation.** Your nsec stays inside one app that you chose deliberately. A client you installed on a whim never sees it, and a client that turns out to be malicious, or that gets breached later, cannot hand over something it never held.

✅ **A moment to look first.** Every signature request appears on screen before anything is published. That catches the accidental post, and it catches a client trying to do something quietly in your name.

✅ **One identity, many apps.** Because the key lives outside the clients, you can use the same account in five different apps without copying your nsec into five different places.

### Popular Signer Apps

**Amber (Android)** is the one to use if you are on Android. Open source, actively developed, holds as many keys as you want, and it is as close to a standard signer as Nostr has.

**Primal (iOS)** works differently, and the difference matters. iOS has no Amber, so Primal keeps your key in the device keychain, inside the client itself. That is a sensible arrangement for iOS and it gives you weaker isolation than a dedicated signer does, because the client and the key holder are the same app. What earns Primal a place in this section is its Remote Login feature: Primal can authorize your activity in other compatible Nostr apps without giving them the key, so those apps get the signer treatment even though Primal itself does not.

Other options:

- **Remote signing over NIP-46**, sometimes called Nostr Connect. This is a capability rather than a product. Services like nsec.app implement it, and so does Primal's Remote Login.
- **Browser extensions.** Alby and nos2x sign for web clients on a desktop.
- **Hardware wallets** (advanced, rarely needed).

### Setting Up Amber

1. **Install it from the right place.** Amber ships through zap.store, F-Droid and GitHub releases (Obtainium will keep it updated from there). It is **not on Google Play**. Anything you find there under that name is somebody else's app, and giving that app your nsec is how an account ends.
2. **Create or import your keys** inside Amber, and back up the recovery words before you go any further.
3. **Open your Nostr client** and choose "Login with Amber", or "external signer", instead of pasting a key anywhere.
4. **Approve the request** when Amber pops up. Android may ask once which app should handle these requests. Pick Amber.

### When to Use Signers

Get a signer once the account matters to you: you post regularly, people follow you for it, or zaps are coming in. The same goes if you move between several clients, because the signer is what makes that safe instead of reckless.

You can skip it while you are still poking around. A throwaway account on a single client does not need the extra step, and a beginner has plenty to learn already. Add it later, when the account has grown into something you would hate to lose.

<Callout variant="warning">
  **Critical:** Your signer app becomes a single point of failure. Back it up
  securely!
</Callout>

## Key Rotation (2 minutes)

### What is Key Rotation?

The word means something different here than it does at work. Nostr has no in-protocol rotation: there is no way to retire a key and carry your identity across to a new one. Your keypair **is** your identity. So "rotating" really means starting a fresh account and persuading everyone to come with you, one follower at a time.

### When to Rotate

Rotate if you think your nsec has escaped. You pasted it into a site that turned out to be a phishing page, a device with the key on it was stolen, someone else has been posting as you. Rotate also when you are leaving a custodial service, meaning one that generated and kept your key for you rather than letting you hold it yourself, because you cannot know who else has a copy.

Do not rotate on a schedule. Yearly rotation is good advice for a work password and bad advice for a Nostr key, because there is no migration path underneath it. Every rotation costs you your posting history, scatters your followers, and breaks the NIP-05 link that other people saved. Rotate when something has actually gone wrong.

### How to Rotate

**Step 1: Prepare**

1. Generate new keys securely
2. Back them up properly
3. Test new account

**Step 2: Announce**

1. Post from old account: "Moving to [new npub]"
2. Pin the post
3. Update bio with new npub
4. Wait a few days

**Step 3: Migrate**

1. Follow your old follows on new account
2. Update NIP-05 if you have one
3. Tell close contacts directly, because that is the step that actually moves people

**Step 4: Sunset**

1. Post from old account periodically: "Moved to [new]"
2. Keep old account as redirect
3. Eventually abandon old account

<Callout variant="warning" title="A NIP-05 does not move your followers">
  You can and should repoint your NIP-05 to the new npub, so anyone looking you
  up by name lands on the right account. It will not migrate the people who
  already follow you. NIP-05 requires clients to keep following the public key
  they originally saved, and to leave that key alone even when the address
  starts answering with a different one. Your existing followers stay pointed at
  the old account until they follow the new one themselves, which is why telling
  them directly is the step that actually works.
</Callout>

## Metadata Leaks (5 minutes)

### What Metadata Leaks?

People are recognizable. Not by their names, by everything else.

Writing style comes first, and creators underestimate it more than anyone. Your vocabulary, your comma habits, whether you capitalize after a colon, the three emoji you actually use out of the thousand available: someone who has read enough of your public work will feel the resemblance in an anonymous post, and software will find it faster than a person can. This is the leak that separate accounts alone do not fix.

Then there is what you say. A street you mentioned in passing. A gig you went to. An in-joke that belongs to one group chat. A fact about your trade that maybe forty people in the world know. No single one identifies you. Three of them together might.

Timing tells people where you live. Post for a month and your active hours sketch out a timezone, then a work schedule, sometimes a school run.

The technical layer leaks quietly, without you doing anything. Every relay you connect to sees your IP address. Some clients stamp their events with the software and version that produced them. Web clients also expose a device fingerprint, which is the particular combination of screen size, fonts, language and settings that makes one browser distinguishable from another even when nobody is logged in.

The social graph is the loudest signal of all. Who you follow, who follows you back, whose posts you reply to within a minute. Two accounts with the same friends are usually the same person, and it takes surprisingly few overlaps before that guess stops being a guess.

### Prevention Strategies

- **Write differently on each account.** Different vocabulary, different sentence rhythm, different punctuation habits. It feels artificial for a week or two and then it stops feeling like anything.
- **Stay vague.** No exact dates, no unique details, generalize the places. "This week" instead of Tuesday, "a city in the north" instead of the neighborhood.
- **Hide your IP.** A VPN keeps relays from seeing where you connect from, and Tor is the stronger version when you need it. Use a different connection for each identity rather than the same one for both.
- **Check what your client sends.** Some tag events with version information. Privacy-focused clients say less about you.
- **Keep the follow graphs apart.** Different people, different circles, and as little contact between your own identities as you can manage.

### Cross-Identity Contamination

Most people who get unmasked are not defeated by clever analysis. They connect the two accounts themselves, once, by accident.

**What NOT to do:**

- ❌ Mention your other account
- ❌ Post same content simultaneously
- ❌ Follow exact same people
- ❌ Use same phrases/slang
- ❌ Respond to same threads
- ❌ Post from both in same conversation

**Safe practices:**

- ✅ Completely separate content
- ✅ Different topics/interests
- ✅ Different active times
- ✅ No cross-references
- ✅ Assume they're watching

<Callout variant="warning">
  **Paranoia check:** If you need true anonymity, assume a motivated adversary
  is analyzing everything. Act accordingly.
</Callout>

## OPSEC Checklist (2 minutes)

### Basic Security (Everyone)

- [ ] Back up keys in 3+ places
- [ ] Use password manager
- [ ] Never screenshot keys
- [ ] Never share nsec
- [ ] Keep client software updated
- [ ] Use unique keys per identity

### Enhanced Security (Active Users)

- [ ] Use signer app
- [ ] Separate devices/accounts
- [ ] VPN/Tor for sensitive posts
- [ ] Rotate keys only if you suspect exposure, never on a calendar
- [ ] Monitor for impersonators
- [ ] Document recovery procedures

### Maximum Security (High Risk)

- [ ] Air-gapped key generation (on a device that never connects to the internet)
- [ ] Hardware security modules (a dedicated chip or device that holds the key and never reveals it)
- [ ] Self-hosted relay
- [ ] Tor-only connections
- [ ] Multiple identity layers
- [ ] Regular OPSEC audits
- [ ] Legal consultation

### Audit Yourself

**Questions to ask:**

1. If someone wanted to dox me, what would they find?
2. Are my multiple identities actually separate?
3. What happens if my phone is stolen?
4. Who would I tell if I got compromised?
5. What's my recovery plan?

## Recovery Planning (2 minutes)

### If You Suspect Compromise

**Immediate actions:**

1. **Stop posting** from compromised account
2. **Document** what happened
3. **Generate new keys** on secure device
4. **Announce** the compromise (if public)
5. **Rotate** to new keys
6. **Investigate** how it happened

### Building a Recovery Plan

**Before you need it:**

1. **Trusted contacts** - Who can verify your new identity?
2. **Proof of identity** - How will you prove you're you?
3. **Out-of-band communication** - How to reach followers if Nostr is compromised?
4. **NIP-05 control** - Can you update your identifier?
5. **Backup strategy** - How are keys backed up?

### Communication Template

Post this from the new account:

```
⚠️ SECURITY ALERT ⚠️

My old account [old npub] has been compromised. Anything
posted from it from today onward is not me.

This is my new account: [new npub]

Please unfollow the old one and follow this one.

You can check it is really me here: [your website, your
other social accounts, or just ask me somewhere else].
```

Then post the mirror image from the old account, naming the new npub, while you still can. Repoint your NIP-05 on a domain you control, since that is the one claim an attacker cannot forge for you. And tell the people who matter through a channel that is not Nostr, because that is what actually convinces anyone.

One thing not to do: do not try to sign the announcement "with both keys". A Nostr event carries exactly one signature, no client will let you attach a second, and once the old key is compromised the attacker can sign with it too, so it would prove nothing even if it were possible.

<Callout variant="info">
  **Hope for the best, plan for the worst.** Having a recovery plan reduces
  panic if something goes wrong.
</Callout>
